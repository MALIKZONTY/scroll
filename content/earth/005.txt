# Earth — batch 5: rocks, minerals, geology & climate
Diamonds form deep underground under extreme heat and pressure, then ride up in volcanic eruptions.
Most diamonds are over a billion years old.
Quartz is one of the most common minerals in Earth's crust.
Granite countertops can contain tiny amounts of naturally radioactive elements. It's harmless at normal levels.
Rocks are grouped into three types: igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic.
Marble is limestone that has been transformed by heat and pressure.
The Taj Mahal is built from Makrana marble from Rajasthan.
Pumice is a volcanic rock so full of air bubbles that it can float.
Obsidian is natural volcanic glass, once used to make blades sharper than steel scalpels.
Fossils form when living things are buried quickly and their remains are replaced by minerals.
Most living things never become fossils. Fossilisation is rare.
Amber can preserve insects for tens of millions of years.
India's Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh are home to fossilised dinosaur egg sites.
The fossil-rich Siwalik Hills in the Himalayan foothills hold remains of ancient elephants, apes and giraffes.
Coal is made from ancient plants that were buried and compressed over millions of years.
Oil and natural gas formed mostly from tiny ancient sea creatures and algae.
Limestone is often made from the skeletons of ancient sea creatures.
The White Cliffs of Dover are made of chalk, the remains of tiny marine algae.
Caves are often carved by slightly acidic water dissolving limestone.
Stalactites hang from cave ceilings. Stalagmites grow up from the floor.
Some stalactites grow only a few millimetres a century.
The largest known cave passage, Hang Son Doong in Vietnam, is big enough to fit a city block inside.
Meghalaya has some of the longest caves in India.
Sand is often made of tiny bits of quartz worn down from rocks over thousands of years.
Black sand beaches are made of volcanic rock.
Pink sand beaches get their colour from tiny red shells mixed with white sand.
Earth's crust is broken into about 15 major tectonic plates.
The Indian plate moves northward at about 5 centimetres a year.
Earthquakes happen when stress between plates suddenly releases.
Most earthquakes happen along the edges of tectonic plates.
Delhi lies in a seismically active zone.
The Richter scale has largely been replaced by the moment magnitude scale for big earthquakes.
Each step up in magnitude means about 32 times more energy released.
Scientists can't yet predict exactly when an earthquake will happen.
Animals sometimes act strangely before earthquakes, but scientists haven't proven a reliable link.
Volcanoes can be active, dormant or extinct.
Mount Vesuvius buried Pompeii in ash in 79 CE, preserving the town for centuries.
Volcanic ash can travel around the world and briefly cool the climate.
The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo cooled global temperatures by about 0.5 °C for a year or so.
Earth has gone through several ice ages in its history.
The last ice age peaked about 20,000 years ago.
During the last ice age, sea levels were about 120 metres lower than today.
During that time, you could have walked from Asia to North America across a land bridge.
Sri Lanka was connected to India by land at times when sea levels were lower.
Adam's Bridge, or Ram Setu, is a chain of shoals between India and Sri Lanka.
Earth may once have been almost completely frozen, a period nicknamed "Snowball Earth."
The air you breathe contains about 0.04% carbon dioxide, but that small amount has a big effect on climate.
Carbon dioxide levels today are higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years, based on ice cores.
The 2010s were the warmest decade on record at the time, and recent years have broken new records.
Earth's average surface temperature has risen by more than 1 °C since the late 1800s.
Sea levels are rising, partly because warmer water expands.
Arctic sea ice in summer has shrunk significantly since satellite records began in 1979.
Glaciers in the Himalayas are losing ice, which affects rivers that billions of people depend on.
The Himalayas are sometimes called the "Third Pole" because they hold so much ice.
Climate and weather are different. Weather is daily. Climate is the long-term average.
Cities are often warmer than the countryside. It's called the urban heat island effect.
Trees in cities can lower street temperatures noticeably on hot days.
White roofs reflect sunlight and can help keep buildings cooler.
Solar power capacity has grown enormously around the world in the last decade.
India has one of the world's largest solar parks, the Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan.
Wind turbines can be taller than many skyscrapers.
Hydropower is the largest source of renewable electricity in the world.
Geothermal energy taps heat from inside the Earth.
Tidal power uses the rise and fall of the ocean to generate electricity.
The ozone hole over Antarctica is slowly healing, thanks to a global ban on harmful chemicals.
The Montreal Protocol, which protects the ozone layer, is one of the most successful environmental agreements ever.
Smog in many cities comes from vehicle exhaust, industry and burning crops or waste.
Air quality can change dramatically with weather: still winter air traps pollution near the ground.
Rain can clean pollutants out of the air.
Earth's water cycle has been recycling the same water for billions of years.
The water in your glass may once have been part of a cloud, a glacier or an ocean.
A water molecule can spend thousands of years in deep groundwater or ice before moving on.
About 30% of Earth's fresh water is underground.
Groundwater is being pumped faster than it refills in many parts of the world, including northern India.
Rainwater harvesting helps refill groundwater.
Stepwells, like the ones in Gujarat and Rajasthan, were built centuries ago to store water.
Rani ki Vav in Gujarat is a stepwell so beautiful it's a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
The Chand Baori stepwell in Rajasthan has about 3,500 steps.
