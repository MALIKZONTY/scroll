# Earth — batch 4: plants, nature & the living planet
Plants make their own food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide through photosynthesis.
There are more than 390,000 known species of plants.
Some plants can "communicate" by releasing chemicals that warn nearby plants of insects.
Trees in a forest can be connected through underground fungal networks, sometimes called the "wood wide web."
Sunflowers face the east as they mature, which may help attract more bees.
Young sunflowers track the Sun across the sky during the day.
The Venus flytrap can count. It snaps shut only if a trigger hair is touched twice in quick succession.
Some carnivorous plants, like pitcher plants, trap insects in pools of digestive liquid.
The corpse flower smells like rotting meat to attract pollinating insects.
The Rafflesia, found in Southeast Asia, has the largest single flower in the world, up to about 1 metre wide.
The Neelakurinji flower in Kerala and Tamil Nadu blooms only once every 12 years.
When Neelakurinji blooms, whole hillsides of the Western Ghats turn blue.
The lotus is India's national flower.
Lotus leaves repel water and dirt so well that scientists copy them for self-cleaning surfaces.
The banyan is India's national tree.
A single banyan tree can grow into what looks like a whole forest.
The Great Banyan in Kolkata's botanical garden covers an area of nearly 2 hectares.
The neem tree has been used for centuries in India for its natural pest-repelling properties.
Bamboo is a grass, not a tree.
Some bamboo species flower all at once only every few decades, then die.
Coconuts can float across oceans and sprout on distant beaches.
The coconut palm is called "kalpavriksha," the tree that gives everything, in parts of India.
Cacti store water in their thick stems to survive in deserts.
The saguaro cactus can live for over 150 years.
Mushrooms are more closely related to animals than to plants.
The largest living organism on Earth may be a honey fungus in Oregon, spread over nearly 10 square kilometres.
Some fungi glow in the dark.
Moss can survive being dried out for years and then come back to life with water.
Lichens are partnerships between fungi and algae or bacteria.
Seeds can stay alive for a very long time. A date palm was grown from a 2,000-year-old seed.
The Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway stores more than a million seed samples as a backup for the world's crops.
A single oak tree can produce thousands of acorns in a good year.
Some trees can live for thousands of years. Others, like many fruit trees, live for decades.
Tree rings can reveal a tree's age and past weather conditions.
Leaves change colour in autumn because the green chlorophyll breaks down, revealing yellows and oranges underneath.
The Amazon is sometimes called "the lungs of the planet," but its plants also use much of the oxygen they produce.
Phytoplankton in the ocean produce a large share of Earth's oxygen.
Kelp forests can grow up to about 50 cm a day.
Seagrass meadows store a lot of carbon and shelter young fish.
Mangroves protect coasts from storm surges and erosion.
Coral reefs protect coastlines by breaking the force of waves.
Coral bleaching happens when warm water stresses corals, making them expel the algae that give them colour.
Wetlands act like sponges, soaking up floodwater.
Bees, butterflies, bats and birds pollinate a large share of the world's food crops.
About three-quarters of leading food crops benefit at least partly from animal pollination.
Earthworms improve soil by mixing it and creating channels for air and water.
A handful of healthy soil can contain more organisms than there are people on Earth.
It can take hundreds of years to form a few centimetres of topsoil.
The Nile, Ganges and Mekong deltas feed hundreds of millions of people.
Rain forests have trees so tall that some animals spend their whole lives in the canopy.
Deserts can bloom with flowers almost overnight after rare rains.
The Atacama Desert sometimes erupts into a carpet of flowers called a "desierto florido."
Fire is natural in some ecosystems. Some pine cones only open to release seeds after a fire.
Volcanic islands like Hawaii start with bare rock, and life slowly arrives by wind, sea and birds.
Islands often have unique species found nowhere else, like the lemurs of Madagascar.
About 90% of Madagascar's wildlife is found nowhere else on Earth.
India is one of the 17 "megadiverse" countries, home to a large share of the world's species.
The Western Ghats are older than the Himalayas.
The Eastern Himalayas have some of the richest plant life in the world.
Loktak Lake in Manipur has floating islands called phumdis.
The Keibul Lamjao National Park on Loktak Lake is the world's only floating national park.
Cherrapunji has living root bridges, grown from rubber fig trees by the Khasi and Jaintia people.
Some living root bridges in Meghalaya are hundreds of years old.
The Valley of Flowers in Uttarakhand bursts with alpine flowers during the monsoon.
The Thar Desert is home to wildlife adapted to heat, like the desert fox and the chinkara.
India's Sundarbans mangroves stretch into Bangladesh.
Forests cover about 31% of the world's land area.
Every year, large areas of forest are lost worldwide, though some regions are regrowing.
Planting the right trees in the right places helps restore ecosystems.
Clean air, clean water and fertile soil are all services nature provides for free.
Many medicines, like aspirin, were inspired by compounds first found in plants.
Aspirin's ancestor came from willow bark, used for pain relief for thousands of years.
Turmeric's yellow colour comes from a compound called curcumin.
Black pepper was once so valuable it was called "black gold."
India's Kerala coast was famous for spices that drew traders from across the world.
Vanilla comes from an orchid.
Chocolate comes from the seeds of the cacao tree.
Coffee beans are actually the seeds of a fruit called a coffee cherry.
Tea, coffee and cocoa plants all produce caffeine, partly as a natural pesticide.
