# Psychology — batch 3: emotions, relationships & self
Emotions usually rise and fall within minutes if you don't keep feeding them with thoughts.
Naming a feeling precisely, like "disappointed" instead of "bad," can help you handle it better.
Suppressing emotions often makes them stronger later.
Crying can help some people feel calmer afterwards.
Anger often hides another feeling underneath, like hurt or fear.
Taking a pause before responding when angry can change the whole outcome of a conversation.
Your body reacts to imagined stress almost the same way it reacts to real stress.
Slow breathing signals safety to your nervous system.
A hug can lower stress for many people.
Physical touch from someone you trust can reduce the feeling of pain in experiments.
Holding hands with a loved one can calm the brain's response to threats, according to one study.
People in close relationships often start to look and sound a bit like each other over time.
Couples who laugh together tend to report more satisfying relationships.
How partners argue matters more than how often they argue.
Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling are warning signs in relationships, according to researcher John Gottman.
Small daily gestures of attention build trust more than big romantic moments.
Turning towards your partner when they share something small builds connection over time.
People tend to fall for those they see often. Proximity matters.
Shared laughter is one of the strongest bonding signals between people.
Eye contact can make people feel more connected.
Attachment styles formed in childhood can influence adult relationships, but they can change.
Feeling butterflies early in a relationship is partly adrenaline.
The early "in love" phase is linked to dopamine, the brain's reward chemical.
Long-term love is linked more with calm, bonding chemicals like oxytocin.
Heartbreak can activate the same brain regions as physical pain.
"Broken heart syndrome" is a real temporary heart condition triggered by extreme stress.
Missing someone can feel like withdrawal, because love involves the brain's reward system.
Rumination, replaying the same painful thoughts, tends to make sadness last longer.
Writing about difficult experiences can help some people process them.
Self-compassion is linked with more resilience, not less motivation.
Perfectionism is often driven by fear of failure.
People-pleasing often comes from wanting to avoid conflict or rejection.
Setting boundaries is linked with healthier relationships.
Saying no doesn't make you a bad person.
Loneliness isn't about how many people are around you. It's about feeling connected.
You can feel lonely in a crowd and content when alone.
Introverts aren't necessarily shy. They often recharge by spending time alone.
Extroverts often recharge by being around people.
Most people are somewhere in between introvert and extrovert. Some call them ambiverts.
Your personality can change over your lifetime.
People tend to become more agreeable and conscientious as they get older.
Confidence often comes after action, not before.
Doing things that scare you a little makes them less scary over time. It's called exposure.
Avoiding what makes you anxious can make the anxiety grow.
Overthinking often feels like problem-solving, but it rarely solves anything.
Writing down your thoughts can help you see them more clearly.
The stories you tell yourself shape how you feel.
Changing how you interpret a situation can change how you feel about it. Psychologists call it reappraisal.
Thinking "this is exciting" instead of "I'm nervous" may help performance, according to some research.
Your inner critic is often harsher than anyone else would be.
Talking to yourself like you'd talk to a friend can reduce stress.
Happiness often comes more from relationships than from money, once basic needs are met.
More money does tend to increase happiness up to a point, and research still debates where that point is.
Buying time, like paying for a chore to be done, can increase happiness for some people.
Spending money on others can make you happier than spending it on yourself.
Anticipating a holiday can bring as much joy as the holiday itself.
People adapt quickly to good and bad changes. It's called hedonic adaptation.
Variety helps fight hedonic adaptation. Small new experiences keep joy fresh.
Savouring a good moment, by pausing to notice it, can make it feel better and last longer.
Comparing yourself to others on social media can lower your mood.
People usually post their best moments online, not their ordinary ones.
Taking breaks from social media can improve mood for some people.
People often feel more anxious when they don't know what to expect.
Routines can reduce stress by making life more predictable.
Helping someone else can lift you out of your own worries.
Volunteering is linked with better well-being.
Having a sense of purpose is linked with better health.
Hope is a skill: setting goals and finding paths to reach them.
Optimism can be learned, at least partly.
People who feel grateful tend to sleep a little better, in some studies.
Laughing with others is far more common than laughing alone.
We laugh far more in conversation than at actual jokes.
Facial expressions for basic emotions are recognised across many cultures, though there are cultural differences.
People can often detect fake smiles. A real smile usually involves the eyes.
A genuine smile, using the muscles around the eyes, is called a Duchenne smile.
When people feel safe, they're more creative and willing to share ideas.
Feeling understood is one of the strongest human needs.
Validation doesn't mean agreeing. It means acknowledging someone's feelings make sense.
People forgive more easily when the apology names what went wrong and how it will change.
A good apology has no "but."
