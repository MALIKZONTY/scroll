# Psychology — batch 4: brain quirks, perception & thinking
Your brain predicts what will happen next, all the time. Surprise is when the prediction fails.
Colours look different depending on the colours around them.
The famous dress photo looked blue-black to some people and white-gold to others because brains guessed the lighting differently.
Your brain edits out your nose from your view, even though it's always there.
You can't see your own eyes move in a mirror because your brain briefly blocks vision during fast eye movements.
Your eyes make several quick jumps every second, called saccades.
Your brain combines two slightly different images from your eyes to create depth.
Optical illusions show that seeing isn't just your eyes. It's your brain interpreting.
The McGurk effect shows that what you see someone's lips do can change what sound you hear.
Your brain can recognise a familiar face in a fraction of a second.
Some people can't recognise faces at all, a condition called prosopagnosia or face blindness.
Synaesthesia is when senses mix, like seeing colours when hearing music.
Some synaesthetes taste words or see numbers as colours.
Phantom vibrations, feeling your phone buzz when it didn't, are very common.
Your brain often hears words in random noise, like your name in the sound of a fan.
A "brain freeze" from cold food is caused by blood vessels in your head reacting to the cold.
The tip-of-the-tongue feeling often clears up if you stop trying and think about something else.
Walking through a doorway can make you forget what you came for. Researchers call it the doorway effect.
Your brain separates life into "events," and doorways can mark the boundary between them.
Daydreaming isn't wasted time. It's linked to creativity and planning.
Your mind wanders a large part of the time you're awake, according to some studies.
People tend to be less happy when their mind wanders than when they're focused, according to one large study.
Flow is the feeling of being so absorbed in something that time disappears.
Flow often happens when a task is challenging but not overwhelming.
Learning is easier when you're curious about the answer.
Curiosity activates the brain's reward system, like anticipating a treat.
Mistakes help learning, especially when you get feedback quickly.
Confidence and competence don't always go together.
Experts often find it hard to explain basics because they've forgotten what it's like to not know. It's called the curse of knowledge.
We often judge decisions by their outcomes, even when the decision itself was good. It's called outcome bias.
After buying something, people tend to focus on its good points. It's called choice-supportive bias.
We tend to prefer options that are presented as the default.
Organ donation rates are much higher in countries where donation is the default.
How a choice is described changes what people pick. "90% survival" sounds better than "10% mortality." It's called framing.
People give more when asked for "even a little" than when no amount is mentioned.
We're more likely to finish something if we can see our progress, like a progress bar.
Loyalty cards that start with a few stamps already filled make people more likely to finish them.
People value things they've built themselves more highly. Researchers call it the IKEA effect.
We tend to think rare events are more likely after we've just heard about one. It's called the availability heuristic.
Plane crashes feel more likely than they are because they get so much news coverage.
People often believe a coin is "due" to land heads after several tails. It isn't. It's called the gambler's fallacy.
Random streaks feel meaningful, but they often happen by chance.
We see patterns in randomness, like shapes in clouds or luck in streaks.
People are more likely to believe a claim if it rhymes. Researchers call it the rhyme-as-reason effect.
Information that's easy to read tends to feel more true.
When people are tired or rushed, they rely more on mental shortcuts.
Most everyday decisions are made quickly and automatically, not by careful reasoning.
Psychologists describe two modes of thinking: fast and intuitive, and slow and deliberate.
Your gut feeling can be right when you have lots of experience in an area.
Making too many decisions in a row may make choices harder later, though the evidence for "decision fatigue" is debated.
Writing a pros and cons list can help you notice what you really care about.
Imagining you're advising a friend can help you make better decisions for yourself.
Distance helps clarity. Thinking about a problem as if it's a year from now can make it feel smaller.
Uncertainty tends to feel worse than bad news.
The brain treats social status and fairness as very important.
People will often reject an unfair offer even if it means getting nothing at all.
Toddlers start helping others spontaneously before they're two years old.
Babies can tell the difference between helpful and unhelpful characters in puppet shows.
Children learn language faster than adults, partly because their brains are more flexible.
Bilingual people can switch between languages very quickly.
Speaking another language can change how people describe themselves.
Music lessons in childhood are linked with some benefits in listening skills.
Reading fiction may help people understand others' feelings.
Imagining a movement can strengthen the brain pathways used to perform it.
Athletes use visualisation to improve performance.
Practice changes the brain. London taxi drivers who learned the city's streets had changes in the hippocampus.
The hippocampus is a brain region crucial for memory and navigation.
Memories become more stable during sleep.
Napping can help learning, especially a short nap after studying.
Caffeine works by blocking a chemical in the brain that makes you feel sleepy.
Sugar doesn't make most children hyperactive, according to many controlled studies.
The idea that we only use 10% of our brains is a myth.
"Left-brained" and "right-brained" personality types are a myth. Both sides work together.
Learning styles, like "visual learners," don't seem to improve learning when matched to teaching.
Goldfish don't actually have a three-second memory. They can remember things for months.
Venting anger by punching a pillow tends to increase anger, not reduce it.
Reading a summary of a book is not the same as reading the book. Details help memory.
Your brain is more alert to change than to things that stay the same.
You stop noticing a constant smell after a while. It's called sensory adaptation.
Your brain ignores the feeling of your clothes on your skin until you think about it. Now you're feeling them.
