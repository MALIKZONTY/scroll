# Psychology — batch 2: everyday mind & behaviour
People tend to remember compliments for a moment but criticism for years. Psychologists call it negativity bias.
Bad experiences often stick in memory more strongly than good ones of the same size.
We tend to think our own opinions are more objective than other people's. It's called naive realism.
People usually like you more than you think after a conversation. Researchers call it the liking gap.
We often underestimate how much a stranger would enjoy talking to us.
Small talk with strangers, like a barista or a fellow commuter, can lift your mood more than people expect.
Remembering someone's name makes them feel valued.
People tend to mirror the body language of those they like.
Your posture can influence how confident you feel, though the effect is smaller than pop science claims.
We judge faces in a fraction of a second.
People who feel lonely often become more alert to social threats, which can make connecting feel harder.
Strong friendships are linked with better health and longer life in many studies.
Gratitude journaling has been linked to small improvements in well-being.
Your mood can change how you remember the past.
When you're anxious, your brain tends to overestimate danger.
Worrying about something rarely changes the outcome, but it does change how you feel now.
The brain tends to prefer certainty, even bad certainty, over not knowing.
People often prefer a sure small reward over a bigger uncertain one.
We tend to value rewards now more than bigger rewards later. Psychologists call it present bias.
The famous marshmallow test showed kids waiting for a second treat. Later research found the context and trust matter a lot.
Self-control isn't just willpower. Changing your environment often works better.
If junk food is out of sight, you're less likely to eat it.
Making a habit easy to start is more important than making it intense.
Writing down a specific plan, like "I'll run at 7 AM in the park," makes you more likely to do it. Researchers call these implementation intentions.
Telling yourself "I don't" instead of "I can't" can make it easier to resist temptations, according to some studies.
Rewards that come right away are more powerful for building habits than distant ones.
Checking your phone often can become a habit loop: cue, action, reward.
Notifications are designed to grab your attention, and your brain finds them hard to ignore.
Each time you switch tasks, it can take time to fully refocus.
Working in focused blocks with short breaks, like the Pomodoro technique, helps some people concentrate.
Your brain doesn't stop working when you rest. Ideas often come during a walk or a shower.
Sleep deprivation can affect your mood and judgement as much as being drunk, in some studies.
Teenagers' body clocks naturally shift later, which is why waking early can feel so hard.
Light from screens late at night can make it harder to fall asleep.
Exercise is one of the most reliable ways to boost mood and reduce stress.
Spending time with pets can lower stress for many people.
Petting a dog can raise levels of oxytocin, a bonding hormone, in both you and the dog.
Music can change your heart rate and breathing.
Familiar songs bring back strong memories because music and memory are tightly linked in the brain.
Smells are strongly tied to memory. A scent can instantly bring back a childhood moment.
The smell of something baking can make people feel happier and even kinder in some studies.
Colours can influence mood, but the effects are smaller and less universal than many claims suggest.
People tend to trust others who speak more slowly and clearly.
Stories are easier to remember than lists of facts.
Teaching something to someone else is one of the best ways to learn it yourself.
You learn better when you try to recall information before checking the answer.
Mixing up different types of problems while studying, called interleaving, can improve learning.
Testing yourself with flashcards beats rereading notes.
Your brain remembers more when you connect new information to something you already know.
Handwriting notes may help some people remember ideas better than typing them word for word.
People often feel more confident than they are accurate. It's called overconfidence bias.
We tend to notice evidence that supports our hopes and ignore the rest.
Once people commit to an opinion publicly, they're less likely to change it.
People are more likely to change their minds when they don't feel attacked.
Asking "how would that work, exactly?" can make people realise they understand less than they thought. It's called the illusion of explanatory depth.
Most people think they know how a zip or a toilet works, until they're asked to explain it step by step.
We tend to believe things we hear repeatedly. It's called the illusory truth effect.
People are more likely to remember information that's surprising.
Humour can make information more memorable.
Feeling awe can make time feel like it's moving more slowly.
Time seems to speed up as you get older, partly because new experiences become rarer.
Trying new things and travelling can make a period of time feel longer in memory.
Waiting feels shorter when you're kept busy or know how long it will take.
Unexplained waits feel longer than explained ones.
We feel more pain from paying with cash than with a card, so we often spend more with cards.
Prices ending in 9, like ₹99, often feel much cheaper than ₹100.
Free samples make people more likely to buy because of a feeling of reciprocity.
When someone does us a favour, we feel an urge to return it. That's the reciprocity principle.
People tend to follow authority figures, even when they shouldn't.
We're more likely to agree with people we like.
Shared struggles bring people closer together.
Doing something novel and exciting together can strengthen a relationship.
Couples who celebrate each other's good news tend to be happier.
How you respond to someone's good news can matter as much as how you respond to bad news.
People feel more connected after sharing something a bit personal.
Asking follow-up questions makes people like you more.
Listening without interrupting is one of the rarest and most valued social skills.
People remember how you made them feel more than what you said.
