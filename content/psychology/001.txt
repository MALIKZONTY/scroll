# Psychology — batch 1 (named, well-documented effects; no invented statistics)
Saying a feeling out loud, like "I'm anxious," can make it feel less intense. Psychologists call it affect labelling.
Unfinished tasks stick in your mind more than finished ones. That's often called the Zeigarnik effect.
Once you learn a new word, you suddenly see it everywhere. That's the frequency illusion, also called the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon.
We tend to believe that other people notice our mistakes far more than they actually do. It's called the spotlight effect.
People who know little about a topic often overestimate their skill. That's known as the Dunning-Kruger effect.
We remember things better when we test ourselves than when we simply reread them. It's called the testing effect.
Spreading out your study sessions helps you remember more than cramming. It's known as the spacing effect.
We tend to remember the first and last items in a list best. That's the serial position effect.
The more you're exposed to something, the more you tend to like it. Psychologists call it the mere exposure effect.
Your brain notices your own name even in a noisy room. It's called the cocktail party effect.
We tend to judge others by their actions but judge ourselves by our intentions.
People often blame others' mistakes on their character, and their own mistakes on the situation. It's called the actor-observer bias.
We tend to search for and remember information that confirms what we already believe. That's confirmation bias.
After an event, people often feel they "knew it all along." That's hindsight bias.
We tend to value things more once we own them. It's called the endowment effect.
Losing something feels worse than gaining the same thing feels good. Psychologists call it loss aversion.
The first number you hear can sway your judgement, even if it's random. That's anchoring.
People are more likely to help when they're alone than when there's a crowd. It's called the bystander effect.
Doing someone a small favour can make you like them more. That's known as the Ben Franklin effect.
People in a group often go along with a bad idea to avoid conflict. That's groupthink.
When you believe something will help you, it can actually make you feel better. That's the placebo effect.
If you expect something to hurt, it can actually make you feel worse. That's the nocebo effect.
Expectations from teachers can affect how well students perform. It's called the Pygmalion effect.
People often work differently when they know they're being watched. It's called the Hawthorne effect.
We tend to think things will take less time than they actually do. That's the planning fallacy.
We often keep investing in something just because we've already put in time or money. That's the sunk cost fallacy.
Your brain can only hold a handful of things in short-term memory at once.
Breaking information into chunks, like a phone number, makes it easier to remember.
Sleep helps your brain store memories. Pulling an all-nighter before an exam can backfire.
Even a short walk can boost your mood.
Writing down your worries before bed can help some people fall asleep faster.
Your brain is more likely to remember emotional moments than ordinary ones.
Memories aren't recordings. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it, and it can change slightly.
People can be made to "remember" events that never happened. Psychologists call these false memories.
We're often happier spending money on experiences than on things.
Helping others can make you feel happier. Some call it the "helper's high."
Smiling can sometimes nudge your mood up a little, even if the effect is small.
Multitasking usually isn't really multitasking. Your brain is switching quickly between tasks and losing time each switch.
Your attention is limited. If you're focused on one thing, you can miss obvious things right in front of you. It's called inattentional blindness.
In a famous experiment, many people counting basketball passes didn't notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene.
When you're hungry, you might be more irritable. "Hangry" is a real thing people experience.
Choice overload is real. Too many options can make it harder to decide and less satisfying when you do.
We tend to like people who are similar to us.
We often see faces in random objects, like clouds or electrical sockets. It's called pareidolia.
Feeling like a fraud despite your success is common. It's called impostor syndrome.
Déjà vu is the strange feeling of having already lived a moment. Scientists still don't fully understand it.
The "tip of the tongue" feeling, when you almost remember a word, happens to almost everyone.
Habits are triggered by cues. Changing your environment can make it easier to change a habit.
Starting with a tiny version of a habit, like one push-up, can make it easier to stick with.
Your brain treats social rejection in some ways similar to physical pain.
Being in nature, even for a short time, may help lower stress.
Listening to music you love can release dopamine, a feel-good chemical in the brain.
Songs that get stuck in your head are called earworms.
People tend to remember interrupted tasks and cliffhangers. That's why TV shows end on them.
We're more likely to agree to a big request after first agreeing to a small one. It's called the foot-in-the-door technique.
People tend to follow what others are doing when they're unsure. That's called social proof.
Things seem more valuable when they're scarce or available for a limited time.
We judge a whole experience mostly by its most intense moment and how it ended. It's called the peak-end rule.
The colour of a pill can affect how people expect it to work.
People often rate themselves above average at things like driving. Not everyone can be above average.
We tend to overestimate how much others agree with us. It's called the false consensus effect.
First impressions can colour everything we think about someone afterwards. It's called the halo effect.
Your brain fills in gaps in what you see. That's why some optical illusions work.
Every eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve connects, and your brain fills it in so you don't notice.
Your brain uses roughly a fifth of your body's energy, even though it's only about 2% of your body weight.
The brain itself can't feel pain. It has no pain receptors.
Naming three things you're grateful for regularly can help improve mood for some people.
Your brain keeps changing throughout your life. It's called neuroplasticity.
Learning a new skill physically changes connections in your brain.
Boredom can actually spark creativity.
People who feel in control of a situation tend to feel less stressed, even when the situation itself doesn't change.
Procrastination is often about avoiding a bad feeling, not about being lazy.
Breaking a big task into tiny steps can make starting much easier.
Deep breathing with longer exhales can help calm your body's stress response.
Talking to yourself in the third person may help you think more calmly about a problem.
Being kind to yourself after a mistake can actually make you more likely to improve.
Feeling awe, like watching a sunset or the night sky, can make people feel more connected and less self-focused.
Laughter is contagious. Hearing others laugh makes you more likely to laugh too.
Yawning is contagious, and even reading about yawning can make you yawn. Did you just yawn?
