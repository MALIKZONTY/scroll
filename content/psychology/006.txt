# Psychology — batch 6: habits, phones, sleep & well-being tips
It takes an average of about two months to form a habit, though it varies a lot between people and habits.
Missing a day doesn't ruin a habit. Consistency over time matters more.
Pairing a new habit with an existing one, like stretching after brushing teeth, helps it stick. It's called habit stacking.
Making a bad habit harder to do, even slightly, can reduce it.
Keeping your phone in another room while studying can improve focus, even if it's switched off.
Just having your phone on the desk can reduce your available brainpower, according to one study.
Turning your phone screen to greyscale makes it less tempting for some people.
Social media apps use variable rewards, like a slot machine, which makes them hard to put down.
Infinite scroll removes natural stopping points, so we keep going.
Setting a specific time to check messages can reduce stress.
Batching emails and messages helps protect focus.
The first hour after waking can set the tone for your day.
Getting morning sunlight can help set your body clock.
A consistent sleep schedule helps you fall asleep more easily.
Caffeine can stay in your system for many hours, so afternoon coffee can affect sleep.
A cool, dark, quiet room helps sleep.
If you can't sleep, getting up and doing something calm until you're sleepy can help.
Writing a to-do list for tomorrow before bed may help some people fall asleep faster.
Naps of 10 to 20 minutes can boost alertness without grogginess.
Long naps can leave you groggy because you wake up from deep sleep.
Dreams happen most vividly during REM sleep.
Most people forget the majority of their dreams within minutes of waking.
Lucid dreaming is when you realise you're dreaming. Some people can learn to do it.
Sleep paralysis is a brief, harmless but scary state between sleep and waking.
Your brain clears out waste more actively during sleep.
Getting enough sleep helps you control your emotions.
Teenagers need more sleep than adults, about 8 to 10 hours.
Regular exercise can improve sleep quality.
Even 10 minutes of exercise can lift your mood.
Exercise helps the brain grow new connections.
Spending time outdoors in green spaces is linked to lower stress.
Looking at nature, even pictures of it, may help the mind recover from mental fatigue.
Cold water on your face can quickly calm your body by triggering a reflex that slows your heart.
Box breathing, in for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four, is used by some people to calm down.
The "5-4-3-2-1" technique, noticing five things you see, four you feel, and so on, can help with anxiety.
Tensing and then relaxing your muscles, called progressive muscle relaxation, can reduce stress.
Talking about worries with someone you trust can make them feel smaller.
Therapy is like a gym for your mind. Going doesn't mean something is "wrong" with you.
Asking for help is a strength.
Mental health is health.
Journalling can help you notice patterns in your moods.
Rereading old journal entries often shows how much you've grown.
People who write down goals are more likely to achieve them, when they also plan the steps.
Celebrating small wins keeps motivation going.
Progress feels motivating, even if it's small. Researchers call it the progress principle.
Tracking a habit visually, like ticking a calendar, can keep you going.
Doing the hardest task first, sometimes called "eating the frog," makes the rest of the day feel easier.
Starting with just 2 minutes can beat procrastination.
Procrastinators often overestimate how unpleasant a task will be.
Promising yourself a small reward after a task can help you start.
Listening to an audiobook only while exercising, called temptation bundling, can make workouts more appealing.
Working near people who are focused can help you focus too.
Background noise, like café sounds, helps some people concentrate.
Music with lyrics can make reading and writing harder for some people.
The colour blue is linked with calm in many people's minds, though effects vary.
Clutter can make it harder for some people to focus.
Tidying up can give a small sense of control on stressful days.
People feel happier when they spend time on things that match their values.
Having something to look forward to boosts mood, even if it's small.
Planning a fun event can bring happiness for weeks before it happens.
Writing a thank-you letter to someone can boost your happiness for weeks.
Practising kindness towards yourself reduces anxiety for many people.
Noticing what went well each day can increase happiness over time.
People tend to remember the end of a holiday more than the middle.
Ending something on a high note makes the whole experience feel better.
Novelty makes memories stick, so trying new things makes life feel richer.
Learning a new skill in adulthood can boost confidence.
Curiosity is linked with life satisfaction.
Hobbies can lower stress by giving your mind a break from worries.
Creative activities, like drawing or playing music, can improve mood.
Laughter can relieve tension.
Pets can provide routine, companionship and comfort.
Dogs can lower stress during exams in student studies.
Hugging for about 20 seconds may help some people feel calmer.
Being around people you trust lowers your body's stress response.
Feeling useful to others is linked to well-being in old age.
Older adults often report being happier than younger adults, despite challenges.
People get better at managing emotions as they age, according to many studies.
Wisdom isn't just age. It grows from reflecting on experiences.
