# Psychology — batch 5: social life, influence & groups
In Solomon Asch's famous experiments, many people gave obviously wrong answers to match the group.
Having even one other person disagree with the group made people far more likely to say what they really saw.
Stanley Milgram's experiments showed people often obey authority figures, even against their conscience.
Later analysis of Milgram's work suggests people's willingness depended a lot on how they saw the purpose of the task.
The Stanford Prison Experiment is famous, but it has been heavily criticised for flawed methods.
People tend to feel more responsible when they're one of few, and less when they're one of many.
Giving someone a specific job in an emergency, like "You in the red shirt, call an ambulance," makes help more likely.
We tend to favour people from our own group, even when groups are formed randomly. It's called in-group bias.
Simply working together on a shared goal can reduce prejudice between groups.
People tend to see members of other groups as more alike than members of their own group.
We often assume others think like us. It's a big source of misunderstandings.
People tend to like others who like them. It's called reciprocal liking.
Similarity in values matters more for long-term friendships than similar hobbies.
Friendships often form among people who see each other regularly, like classmates or neighbours.
It takes many hours together to turn an acquaintance into a close friend, according to one study.
People feel closer after doing something slightly vulnerable together, like sharing a fear.
In one study, strangers who answered 36 increasingly personal questions together felt surprisingly close afterwards.
Gossip may have helped early humans keep track of who could be trusted.
People share good news more readily than bad news about themselves, but bad news spreads faster in many settings.
False news spreads faster than true news on social media, according to a large study of Twitter.
Emotional posts get shared more than neutral ones.
Outrage spreads especially fast online.
People feel more certain about opinions they've stated in public.
Changing someone's mind works better with questions than with arguments.
People are more likely to accept a message if it comes from someone they see as part of their group.
Fear-based messages can backfire if people don't know what to do about the threat.
Telling people "most others do this" can nudge behaviour, like reusing hotel towels.
Small nudges, like putting fruit at eye level, can change choices without banning anything.
People often follow the crowd on social media, liking posts that already have many likes.
Reviews and ratings strongly influence what we buy, even from strangers.
Influencers feel like friends to many followers. Psychologists call it a parasocial relationship.
Parasocial relationships are normal and can even provide comfort.
Celebrities' recommendations can change behaviour, sometimes in big ways.
People are more generous when they feel watched, even by a picture of eyes.
Sharing a meal builds trust and cooperation.
Laughing together makes people feel more connected and more willing to share.
Synchronised activities, like dancing or singing in a group, can increase bonding.
Team sports can boost a sense of belonging.
Being part of a community is linked to better mental health.
People in collectivist cultures tend to describe themselves more in terms of relationships and roles.
People in individualist cultures tend to describe themselves more in terms of personal traits.
Personal space preferences differ across cultures.
Eye contact norms vary around the world.
Nodding means "yes" in many places, but in some regions a head wobble or shake carries different meanings.
The Indian head wobble can mean yes, okay, maybe or "I understand," depending on context.
Politeness rules vary across languages, like formal and informal forms of "you."
Hindi has three levels of "you": tu, tum and aap.
People tend to trust faces that look happy and slightly familiar.
Attractive people are often assumed to be kinder or smarter. That's the halo effect at work.
Names can shape first impressions, which is unfair, but researchers have measured it.
People judge competence partly from how confident someone sounds.
Speaking slightly slower and pausing can make you sound more confident.
Uptalk, ending sentences like questions, can make people sound less sure, even when they're not.
Using someone's name in conversation can make them feel more engaged.
Saying "because" with a reason makes people more likely to agree to a small request, even if the reason is weak.
Asking for advice makes people feel valued and can build rapport.
People like you more when you ask them follow-up questions.
Being a good listener is often rated as more attractive than being a good talker.
People overestimate how awkward a deep conversation with a stranger will be.
Talking to strangers on a commute made people happier in experiments, even though they predicted it wouldn't.
We underestimate how much others appreciate a thank-you note.
People underestimate how happy a compliment makes others feel.
Random acts of kindness make both the giver and receiver happier.
Kindness can be contagious, spreading through social networks.
Happiness can spread through social networks too, according to some research.
Feeling lonely is as common among young adults as older adults, according to some surveys.
Social media can make people feel both connected and lonely.
Face-to-face conversations tend to build closeness more than texting.
Voice calls make people feel more connected than texts, according to one study.
People feel more connected after phone calls than they expect.
Being left out, even in a simple online ball-tossing game, hurts in experiments.
Belonging is a basic human need.
People who feel they belong are more likely to keep going when things get hard.
Feeling like an outsider can affect performance, a pattern linked to stereotype threat.
Reminding people of their values before a stressful task can help them perform better.
Mentors and role models who look like us can make big goals feel more possible.
Leaders who admit mistakes are often trusted more, not less.
Teams perform better when members feel safe speaking up. Researchers call it psychological safety.
Groups of diverse thinkers often solve problems better than groups of similar experts.
Brainstorming alone first, then sharing, often generates more ideas than group brainstorming.
The loudest person in a meeting isn't necessarily the most right.
