# Space — batch 6: Sun, Moon, Earth & sky-watching basics
The Sun is a nearly perfect sphere.
The Sun is made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
The Sun spins faster at its equator than at its poles, because it's not solid.
The Sun takes about 25 days to spin once at its equator.
The Sun's surface temperature is about 5,500 °C.
The Sun produces energy by fusing hydrogen into helium.
The Sun is so massive that it holds the whole solar system together with its gravity.
The Sun's gravity reaches far beyond Pluto, holding the Oort Cloud in orbit.
Without the Sun, Earth's surface would freeze within weeks to months.
Solar eclipses can only happen at new moon. Lunar eclipses can only happen at full moon.
We don't get an eclipse every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted.
A total solar eclipse is visible from any given place only once every few hundred years, on average.
During a total solar eclipse, the temperature can drop noticeably and birds may go quiet.
An annular eclipse happens when the Moon is too far away to cover the Sun completely, leaving a "ring of fire."
The Moon's phases happen because we see different amounts of its sunlit side.
A "supermoon" is a full moon that happens near the Moon's closest approach to Earth.
A supermoon can look about 14% bigger and 30% brighter than a full moon at its farthest point.
The Moon looks bigger near the horizon, but that's an illusion. Its size doesn't change.
The Moon's craters are mostly from ancient impacts, preserved because there's no weather.
The Moon has no global magnetic field today.
The Moon causes most of Earth's ocean tides.
The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, on average.
A "waxing" moon is growing; a "waning" moon is shrinking.
Earth's shadow is visible at sunset as a dark blue band on the opposite horizon, called the Earth's shadow, topped by a pink "Belt of Venus."
Earthshine is when sunlight reflects off Earth and faintly lights up the dark part of the Moon.
You can see the ISS, satellites and even Starlink trains with your naked eye on clear nights.
Satellites are visible because they reflect sunlight, usually just after sunset or before sunrise.
A "planet parade" is when several planets appear lined up across the sky.
Mercury is hard to see because it's always close to the Sun in our sky.
Mars appears reddish to the naked eye, even from Earth.
Saturn looks like a steady golden star to the naked eye.
Uranus can be just barely seen with the naked eye in very dark skies, if you know exactly where to look.
Neptune can't be seen without a telescope.
The zodiacal light is a faint glow of sunlight scattered by dust in the solar system, seen after dusk or before dawn.
Light pollution makes it hard to see the Milky Way from most cities.
Ladakh's Hanle has India's first Dark Sky Reserve.
The Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle is one of the highest observatories in the world.
Your eyes take about 20 to 30 minutes to fully adjust to the dark.
Using red light helps you keep your night vision while stargazing.
Binoculars can show you craters on the Moon and Jupiter's moons.
The best meteor showers can produce dozens of meteors an hour in dark skies.
Meteors usually burn up about 80 to 120 km above the ground.
Fireballs are especially bright meteors, sometimes brighter than Venus.
Sky-watchers in ancient India named the 27 nakshatras, or lunar mansions.
Jantar Mantar in Jaipur has the world's largest stone sundial, accurate to about 2 seconds.
Jantar Mantar observatories were built by Maharaja Jai Singh II in the 1700s.
Aryabhata proposed that Earth rotates on its axis, around 499 CE.
India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was launched in 1975.
ISRO stands for the Indian Space Research Organisation, founded in 1969.
Vikram Sarabhai is considered the father of the Indian space programme.
India's first rocket launch, in 1963, was from Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram.
Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh is home to ISRO's main launch centre.
NASA was founded in 1958.
The European Space Agency was founded in 1975.
The first image of Earth from space was taken in 1946 from a rocket.
"Earthrise," taken by Apollo 8 in 1968, is one of the most famous photos ever taken.
"The Blue Marble" photo was taken by the Apollo 17 crew in 1972.
From the Moon, Earth looks about four times wider than the Moon looks from Earth.
Earth doesn't rise and set in the sky as seen from the Moon's near side. It stays in roughly the same spot.
A year on Earth is about 365.25 days, which is why we have leap years.
Earth isn't the same distance from the Sun all year. Its orbit is slightly oval.
Earth moves fastest in its orbit in early January, when it's closest to the Sun.
The seasons are opposite in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
On the equinoxes, day and night are nearly equal everywhere.
The summer solstice in the Northern Hemisphere is the longest day of the year, around 21 June.
At the North Pole, there's one long day and one long night each year.
Earth's atmosphere protects us from most meteors and harmful radiation.
Earth's magnetic field creates the aurora by guiding solar particles towards the poles.
Strong solar storms can push auroras far from the poles. In 2024, auroras were seen as far south as Ladakh.
Earth spins at about 1,670 km/h at the equator, but you don't feel it because everything moves with you.
If Earth stopped spinning suddenly, everything not attached to bedrock would be flung eastward. Luckily, that can't happen.
Earth is the only planet in the solar system with liquid water on its surface.
Earth is the only planet known to have life.
Earth is the fifth-largest planet in the solar system.
Earth's orbit around the Sun is about 940 million km long.
There are thousands of pieces of tracked space debris larger than a softball orbiting Earth.
The Kessler syndrome is a scenario where space debris collisions cause a chain reaction.
The first piece of space debris was Sputnik's rocket body, in 1957.
Some satellites are placed in geostationary orbit, about 36,000 km up, where they stay over the same spot.
GPS uses about 30 satellites orbiting about 20,000 km above Earth.
India's NavIC is its own regional satellite navigation system.
India's INSAT satellites help with weather forecasting and TV broadcasting.
Weather satellites let forecasters track cyclones days before landfall.
Satellites can measure sea level, forest cover and even crop health from space.
Some satellites can see objects smaller than a metre from orbit.
