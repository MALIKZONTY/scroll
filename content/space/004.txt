# Space — batch 4: stars, galaxies & the night sky
Stars twinkle because their light is bent by moving layers of Earth's atmosphere.
Planets usually don't twinkle as much as stars, because they appear as tiny discs rather than points.
Polaris, the North Star, isn't the brightest star in the sky. It's just almost directly above Earth's North Pole.
Polaris won't always be the North Star. Earth's wobble slowly shifts which star sits above the pole.
In about 12,000 years, the bright star Vega will be close to the North Star position.
Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky.
Sirius is actually two stars: a bright one and a small white dwarf companion.
Many stars in the sky are part of systems with two or more stars.
The Sun is a yellow dwarf star, technically classified as a G-type star.
The Sun isn't really yellow. From space, its light looks white.
Red giants are old stars that have swelled up as they run low on fuel.
The Orion Nebula is a star nursery where new stars are being born. You can see it with binoculars.
Nebulae are giant clouds of gas and dust in space.
The Pillars of Creation are towering columns of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula, photographed by Hubble and Webb.
The Pleiades, also called the Seven Sisters, is a cluster of young stars visible to the naked eye.
In India, the Pleiades are known as Krittika.
The Big Dipper, called Saptarishi in India, is part of the constellation Ursa Major.
There are 88 officially recognised constellations.
Constellations are patterns we see from Earth. The stars in them are often vastly different distances away.
The zodiac constellations are the ones the Sun appears to pass through during the year.
The Milky Way looks like a band across the sky because we're inside a flat, disc-shaped galaxy.
From a truly dark place, you can see a few thousand stars with your eyes.
Light pollution hides the Milky Way from most of the world's city dwellers.
The Milky Way has spiral arms, and our Sun sits in a smaller arm called the Orion Arm.
Our solar system is about 26,000 light-years from the centre of the galaxy.
The Andromeda Galaxy is the most distant thing most people can see with their bare eyes, about 2.5 million light-years away.
The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are small galaxies that orbit the Milky Way, visible from the Southern Hemisphere.
Galaxies come in different shapes: spiral, elliptical and irregular.
Some galaxies have a bar of stars across their centre. The Milky Way is one of them.
The biggest galaxies can contain trillions of stars.
Galaxy clusters can hold thousands of galaxies bound together by gravity.
Most galaxies have a supermassive black hole at their centre.
Black holes aren't cosmic vacuum cleaners. If the Sun became a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting normally.
The first black hole ever confirmed was Cygnus X-1, studied in the 1970s.
Sagittarius A*, the black hole at our galaxy's centre, was photographed for the first time in 2022.
Pulsars are spinning neutron stars that sweep beams of radio waves across space like lighthouses.
The first pulsar signal was so regular that scientists jokingly labelled it "LGM-1," for "little green men."
Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovered the first pulsar in 1967 as a graduate student.
Magnetars are neutron stars with the strongest magnetic fields known in the universe.
A supernova happens roughly once or twice a century in a galaxy like ours.
The last supernova clearly seen in our own galaxy was in 1604, observed by Johannes Kepler.
Supernova 1987A, in a nearby galaxy, was visible to the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere.
White dwarfs are the leftover cores of stars like the Sun.
When neutron stars collide, they can create gold, platinum and other heavy elements.
In 2017, astronomers saw a neutron star collision with both gravitational waves and light for the first time.
Gamma-ray bursts are the most energetic explosions known in the universe.
Brown dwarfs are "failed stars," too small to sustain fusion like the Sun.
Some stars move so fast they're being flung out of the galaxy. They're called hypervelocity stars.
Variable stars change brightness over time. Some pulse like a heartbeat.
Astronomers use certain pulsing stars, called Cepheids, to measure distances to galaxies.
Edwin Hubble used Cepheid stars to prove that other galaxies exist beyond the Milky Way.
Before the 1920s, many astronomers thought the Milky Way was the entire universe.
The first known exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star was found in 1995.
Some exoplanets are "hot Jupiters," giant planets orbiting very close to their stars.
Kepler-452b was nicknamed "Earth's cousin" because it orbits a Sun-like star in its habitable zone.
The habitable zone is the region around a star where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface.
A meteor shower happens when Earth passes through debris left by a comet.
The Perseids peak every August. The Geminids peak every December.
The Geminid meteor shower comes from an asteroid, 3200 Phaethon, not a comet.
Meteorites that reach the ground are often older than any rock on Earth.
Some meteorites found on Earth came from Mars, blasted off by ancient impacts.
The Hoba meteorite in Namibia is the largest known single meteorite, about 60 tonnes.
A meteor that exploded over Chelyabinsk, Russia, in 2013 shattered windows across the city.
An asteroid impact about 66 million years ago is widely linked to the extinction of the dinosaurs.
The crater from that impact, Chicxulub, lies partly under Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula.
NASA tracks thousands of near-Earth asteroids to check for any danger.
Comets are often called "dirty snowballs" made of ice, dust and rock.
A comet's tail can stretch for millions of kilometres.
Comet Hale-Bopp was visible to the naked eye for a record 18 months in 1996–97.
Astronomers can tell what stars are made of by splitting their light into colours, a method called spectroscopy.
Helium was discovered in the Sun's light before it was found on Earth. Its name comes from Helios, the Greek Sun god.
The Sun has an 11-year cycle of activity, with more sunspots and solar flares at its peak.
Sunspots look dark only because they're cooler than the surrounding surface.
Huge solar storms can disrupt satellites, GPS and power grids on Earth.
The Carrington Event of 1859 was a solar storm so strong that telegraph wires sparked.
The Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is millions of degrees, far hotter than its surface. Scientists are still working out why.
A total solar eclipse is the only time most people can see the Sun's corona with their eyes.
Never look at the Sun directly, even during most of an eclipse. Use proper eclipse glasses.
Venus and Jupiter are often the brightest "stars" you'll see in the evening sky. They're planets.
You can see Saturn's rings with a small backyard telescope.
You can see Jupiter's four biggest moons with ordinary binoculars.
Galileo discovered those four moons in 1610. They're called the Galilean moons.
