# Time & universe — batch 7: top-up
A light-second is about 300,000 km. A light-minute is about 18 million km.
The Sun is about 8.3 light-minutes away.
Jupiter is between about 33 and 54 light-minutes away, depending on where the planets are.
The edge of the solar system, where the Oort Cloud may end, could be over a light-year away.
The Milky Way is about 13.6 billion years old, nearly as old as the universe.
The oldest stars in our galaxy formed shortly after the Big Bang.
The universe was about 3,000 kelvin, roughly 2,700 °C, when the cosmic background light was released.
Since then, the universe has cooled to about −270 °C.
In the very early universe, all four fundamental forces may have been unified as one.
Inflation is a theory that the universe expanded incredibly fast in its first tiny fraction of a second.
During inflation, the universe may have grown by a factor of at least 10²⁶ in a tiny fraction of a second.
Protons and neutrons formed within the first second after the Big Bang.
The first atomic nuclei formed within the first few minutes.
The first atoms formed about 380,000 years after the Big Bang.
The "cosmic dark ages" were the period before the first stars switched on.
The first galaxies were small and messy, then merged into bigger ones.
Our Sun is a third-generation star, made from the remains of earlier stars.
Galaxies are moving apart, but nearby galaxies can still collide because gravity wins at short distances.
The universe's expansion rate is called the Hubble constant.
Different methods give slightly different values for the Hubble constant. It's called the "Hubble tension."
Nobody knows what dark energy is. It's one of physics' greatest mysteries.
If dark energy grows stronger, the universe might one day tear apart in a "Big Rip." Most scientists think this is unlikely.
Another possibility is a "Big Freeze," where everything slowly cools and fades.
A "Big Crunch," where the universe collapses back, seems unlikely based on current data.
Time can be measured with the decay of radioactive atoms. That's how scientists date rocks.
Carbon dating works for things up to about 50,000 years old.
To date older rocks, scientists use elements with much longer half-lives, like uranium.
Tree rings, ice cores and sediment layers are all natural clocks.
Some ice cores reveal Earth's climate going back over 800,000 years.
Counting layers in lake sediments can reveal yearly changes over thousands of years.
Light from the Andromeda Galaxy left it before humans existed.
Some of the stars in Orion's Belt are hundreds of light-years away, others over a thousand.
The light from Betelgeuse that you see tonight left it around 500 to 700 years ago.
If Betelgeuse explodes, it will be visible in daylight for weeks, but it's far enough away to be safe.
There's no universal "now" in space. Your "now" and a distant alien's "now" can't be compared simply.
A spaceship travelling at 99.99999% of light speed could cross the galaxy within a human lifetime, from the crew's point of view.
For everyone back on Earth, that same trip would take over 100,000 years.
The faster you travel, the shorter distances in the direction of motion appear. It's called length contraction.
Relativity has been tested in countless experiments, and it has passed every one so far.
Einstein's predictions of gravitational waves took about 100 years to confirm.
Quantum mechanics and relativity are both incredibly well tested, yet they don't fit together.
Physicists hope a "theory of everything" will one day unite all the forces.
Every second, the universe gets a little bigger, a little older and a little more mysterious.
The universe has been around for 13.8 billion years, and you get to experience a tiny, precious slice of it.
In the grand scale of time, every human life is short, which is exactly what makes it valuable.
Look up tonight. You're seeing the past, standing in the present and travelling into the future at one second per second.
Time is the only thing we all spend at exactly the same rate: 60 seconds a minute.
