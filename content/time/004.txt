# Time & universe — batch 4: history of time, calendars & clocks
Ancient Egyptians divided day and night into 12 hours each. That's partly why we have 24 hours.
We have 60 minutes in an hour because of the ancient Babylonians, who counted in base 60.
The Babylonian base-60 system is also why a circle has 360 degrees.
Sundials were among the first clocks, used thousands of years ago.
Water clocks, called clepsydras, measured time by how fast water dripped.
Hourglasses were used on ships to measure time and speed.
Mechanical clocks appeared in Europe in the late 1200s.
Early clocks often had only an hour hand.
The pendulum clock, developed by Christiaan Huygens in 1656, made clocks far more accurate.
Before standard time, every town set its clocks by its own local noon.
Greenwich Mean Time became the world's reference in the 1880s.
The Prime Meridian passes through Greenwich in London.
Indian Standard Time is based on a meridian passing near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh.
Before IST, India had separate Bombay Time and Calcutta Time.
Daylight saving time was proposed partly to save energy. India doesn't use it.
Some countries have time zones offset by 30 or 45 minutes, like India and Nepal.
Nepal's time zone is 5 hours and 45 minutes ahead of UTC.
The International Date Line zigzags to avoid splitting countries into two days.
You can celebrate New Year twice by flying east across the Date Line on New Year's Eve.
Samoa skipped 30 December 2011 entirely when it moved across the Date Line.
Kiribati is among the first places on Earth to welcome each new year.
A "week" of seven days likely comes from the seven visible moving objects in the sky: Sun, Moon and five planets.
Many weekday names come from those sky objects. "Sunday" is the Sun's day and "Monday" the Moon's.
In Hindi, weekdays are named after celestial bodies too: Ravivar for the Sun, Somvar for the Moon.
The month of July is named after Julius Caesar, and August after Augustus.
September, October, November and December mean seven, eight, nine and ten in Latin. They were once months 7 to 10.
The Roman calendar originally had just 10 months.
February has 28 days partly because of Roman superstitions about even numbers.
India's national calendar, the Saka calendar, was officially adopted in 1957 alongside the Gregorian calendar.
Many Indian festivals follow lunar or lunisolar calendars, so their dates shift each year.
The Islamic calendar is lunar, so its months move through the seasons over about 33 years.
The Chinese calendar names each year after one of 12 animals.
The Mayan calendar didn't predict the end of the world in 2012. It just started a new cycle.
A year isn't exactly 365.25 days. It's about 365.2422 days.
Without leap years, the calendar would drift by about a day every four years.
People born on 29 February only get a true birthday every four years.
The chance of being born on 29 February is about 1 in 1,461.
The year 2100 will not be a leap year.
A decade, a century and a millennium are 10, 100 and 1,000 years.
The 21st century technically began on 1 January 2001, not 2000, because there was no year zero.
There's no year 0 in the Gregorian calendar. 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE.
Atomic clocks keep time using the vibrations of atoms.
The world's official time, UTC, is calculated from hundreds of atomic clocks around the world.
Leap seconds are being phased out by 2035, according to an international decision in 2022.
Earth's rotation has recently sped up slightly, giving some of the shortest days on record.
The shortest day measured by atomic clocks, in 2022, was about 1.6 milliseconds shorter than 24 hours.
A big earthquake can very slightly change the length of Earth's day.
The 2011 Japan earthquake shortened the day by about 1.8 microseconds, by one estimate.
Melting glaciers can change Earth's spin very slightly, a bit like a figure skater moving their arms.
Your phone's clock syncs with highly accurate time servers on the internet.
GPS satellites carry atomic clocks on board.
Computers count time as the number of seconds since 1 January 1970 in many systems. It's called Unix time.
Some old computer systems will run out of numbers to count time in 2038. It's called the Year 2038 problem.
The Y2K bug worried the world in 1999 because many computers stored years with only two digits.
Clocks in films and adverts are often set to about 10:10 because it looks like a smile and frames the logo.
Big Ben is actually the name of the bell, not the clock tower.
The clock tower housing Big Ben was renamed the Elizabeth Tower in 2012.
Rajabai Clock Tower in Mumbai was modelled on Big Ben.
Some animals and plants have internal clocks so precise they can track time without seeing the sun.
Your body clock runs on a cycle of roughly 24 hours, called a circadian rhythm.
The 2017 Nobel Prize in Medicine was awarded for discoveries about how our body clocks work.
Jet lag happens when your body clock is out of sync with local time.
Flying east usually causes worse jet lag than flying west.
Time can feel slower when you're scared, but experiments suggest your brain doesn't actually process faster. It just remembers more.
Time feels faster as we age, partly because each year is a smaller fraction of your life.
A minute of plank feels much longer than a minute of scrolling.
You can't tickle time, save it in a box or get it back. But you can choose how to spend it.
Some cultures see time as a cycle rather than a straight line.
In Hindu cosmology, time moves in vast cycles called yugas.
A "kalpa" in Hindu cosmology is said to last 4.32 billion years, curiously close to Earth's age.
The phrase "once in a blue moon" refers to a rare extra full moon.
A blue moon happens roughly every two to three years.
A "fortnight" is two weeks, from the old English "fourteen nights."
A "moment" was once a medieval unit of time equal to about 90 seconds.
The word "hour" comes from the Greek "hora," meaning season or time.
"Calendar" comes from the Latin "kalendae," the first day of the Roman month.
"Clock" comes from words for "bell," because early clocks rang bells instead of showing a face.
