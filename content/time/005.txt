# Time & universe — batch 5: deep time & big numbers
Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago.
The first simple life appeared on Earth at least 3.5 billion years ago.
For most of Earth's history, life was microscopic.
Oxygen built up in Earth's air about 2.4 billion years ago, thanks to tiny ocean microbes.
That rise of oxygen, the Great Oxidation Event, may have wiped out many early microbes.
Complex animals appeared in a burst about 540 million years ago, called the Cambrian explosion.
Plants moved onto land about 470 million years ago.
The first forests grew about 385 million years ago.
Dinosaurs ruled for about 165 million years.
Tyrannosaurus rex lived closer in time to humans than to Stegosaurus.
Birds are living dinosaurs.
Mammals lived alongside dinosaurs, but most were small.
The age of mammals began after the dinosaurs disappeared, about 66 million years ago.
Grass is surprisingly young. Grasslands spread widely only in the last few tens of millions of years.
The Himalayas started forming around 50 million years ago.
Our human ancestors split from chimpanzee ancestors about 6 to 7 million years ago.
Homo sapiens appeared roughly 300,000 years ago.
Humans reached the Indian subcontinent at least 50,000 to 70,000 years ago.
Farming began about 12,000 years ago.
The oldest known cave paintings are over 40,000 years old.
The Bhimbetka rock shelters in Madhya Pradesh have rock art that may be thousands of years old.
Writing appeared about 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia and Egypt.
The Great Pyramid was built about 4,500 years ago.
Stonehenge was built in stages starting about 5,000 years ago.
The Indus Valley cities flourished about 4,500 years ago.
The Iron Age began around 3,000 years ago in many regions.
All of written history is only a few thousand years long.
If Earth's 4.5 billion years were a 24-hour clock, humans would arrive in the last few seconds before midnight.
On that clock, dinosaurs would appear at about 10:47 PM and vanish at about 11:39 PM.
On that same clock, life would start before 4 AM.
A million seconds is under 12 days. A billion seconds is nearly 32 years. A trillion seconds is about 31,700 years.
If you lived to 80, you'd have lived about 2.5 billion seconds.
Your heart will beat around 3 billion times in an average lifetime.
You'll spend about a third of your life asleep.
You'll blink millions of times a year.
The Sun has orbited the galaxy only about 20 times since it formed.
The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, roughly 3 times as old as Earth.
Some stars that formed early in the universe are still shining today.
The Sun will keep shining roughly as it is now for billions of years.
In about a billion years, the Sun will be bright enough to boil away Earth's oceans.
In about 5 billion years, the Sun may swell enough to swallow Mercury and Venus, and possibly Earth.
After that, the Sun will shrink into a white dwarf and slowly fade for trillions of years.
Red dwarf stars will still be shining long after the Sun is gone.
The last stars in the universe may fade after about 100 trillion years.
After that, the universe may enter long eras dominated by black holes.
The biggest black holes could take around a googol years to evaporate.
A googol is 10¹⁰⁰: a 1 with 100 zeros.
Scientists estimate there are about 10⁸⁰ atoms in the observable universe.
The number of possible chess games is far larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe.
Earth's beaches hold roughly 10¹⁸ to 10¹⁹ grains of sand, by one estimate.
The Moon has been slowly moving away from Earth for billions of years.
When the dinosaurs lived, the Moon was a bit closer and days were shorter.
Coral fossils record how many days there were in a year long ago. In some periods, a year had around 400 days.
Earth's continents will likely merge again into a new supercontinent in roughly 200 to 250 million years.
Scientists have nicknamed that future supercontinent names like Pangaea Ultima or Amasia.
The Atlantic Ocean is getting wider by a few centimetres every year.
The Mediterranean Sea once dried up almost completely, about 6 million years ago.
The Sahara turns green roughly every 20,000 years because of a wobble in Earth's orbit.
Earth's orbit changes shape and tilt in long cycles, called Milankovitch cycles, which help drive ice ages.
Earth's axis wobbles like a spinning top, completing a circle about every 26,000 years.
That wobble means the zodiac constellations behind the Sun have shifted since ancient times.
Light from the Sun's surface is 8 minutes old. Light from the nearest star is over 4 years old. Light from the Andromeda Galaxy is 2.5 million years old.
Looking deeper into space means looking further back in time.
The James Webb Space Telescope has seen galaxies as they were just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.
The cosmic microwave background shows the universe as it was about 380,000 years after the Big Bang.
We can't see beyond that time with light, because the universe was opaque before it.
Gravitational waves or neutrinos might one day let us see even earlier.
Some scientists think time itself began with the Big Bang, so asking "what came before" might not make sense.
Other ideas suggest our universe could have come from an earlier one. There's no evidence yet either way.
Time might be emergent, arising from something deeper, according to some physicists.
In relativity, past, present and future may all exist equally, an idea called the block universe.
Nobody knows whether time travel to the past is possible. Physics hasn't ruled it out completely, but no one knows how.
Travelling to the future is possible, in a sense: move very fast or sit near a strong gravity source, and you'll age slower.
Cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev, after over 800 days in orbit, travelled about 1/50th of a second into the future compared with people on Earth.
The longest-lived individual trees and organisms are older than many ancient civilisations.
Some bacteria found in ancient salt crystals may have been revived after millions of years, though this is debated.
