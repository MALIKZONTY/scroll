# Time & universe — batch 1 (well-established physics and cosmology)
Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach you. Every time you see the Sun, you're looking 8 minutes into the past.
Time runs slightly faster on a mountaintop than at sea level. Gravity slows time.
GPS satellites have to correct for relativity every day. Without it, positions would drift by about 10 km a day.
Clocks on GPS satellites tick about 38 microseconds a day faster than clocks on the ground.
The faster you move, the slower time passes for you compared with someone standing still. It's called time dilation.
Astronauts on the ISS age very slightly slower than people on Earth, by a few thousandths of a second over six months.
Nothing with mass can travel as fast as light in a vacuum.
Light travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. It could circle Earth about 7.5 times in one second.
Light from the Moon takes about 1.3 seconds to reach Earth. You see the Moon as it was a moment ago.
When you look at the Andromeda Galaxy, you're seeing light that left it about 2.5 million years ago.
The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago with the Big Bang.
The universe is expanding, and that expansion is speeding up.
Distant galaxies are moving away from us faster than light. Space itself is stretching, so it doesn't break the speed limit.
The faint glow left over from the Big Bang, the cosmic microwave background, is everywhere in the sky.
Some of the static on an old analogue TV came from the cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang.
For the first roughly 380,000 years, the universe was so hot and dense that light couldn't travel freely.
The first stars probably formed a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.
The atoms in your body were forged in stars that exploded long before the Sun existed. You are literally made of star stuff.
The iron in your blood was made inside dying stars.
Gold on Earth was likely created in violent events like colliding neutron stars.
If you compressed all of human history into one 24-hour day of Earth's history, humans would appear in the last few seconds.
If Earth's whole history were one calendar year, dinosaurs would vanish around 26 December and humans would show up on 31 December, late in the evening.
A second is officially defined by vibrations of the caesium atom: 9,192,631,770 of them.
Atomic clocks are so accurate they would lose less than a second in millions of years.
The best optical clocks are so precise they can detect the change in time when lifted just a few centimetres higher.
Leap seconds have been added to clocks every so often because Earth's rotation isn't perfectly steady.
We add a leap day almost every four years because a year is about 365.24 days long.
Years divisible by 100 aren't leap years, unless they're also divisible by 400. That's why 2000 was a leap year but 1900 wasn't.
Black holes stretch time. Near one, time would pass much more slowly than far away.
At the event horizon of a black hole, light can't escape no matter what.
Black holes slowly lose energy through Hawking radiation, but for big ones it would take far longer than the current age of the universe.
Gravitational waves are ripples in space and time. They were first detected directly in 2015.
The gravitational waves detected in 2015 came from two black holes that collided over a billion years ago.
The Sun's light you see today left its surface about 8 minutes ago, but the energy was created deep in its core long before that.
Stars you see at night might not even exist anymore. Their light could still be travelling to you.
The Big Bang wasn't an explosion in space. It was the expansion of space itself.
There is no centre of the universe. Everything is moving away from everything else.
The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across, but the whole universe could be much larger, or infinite.
In about 4 to 5 billion years, the Milky Way and Andromeda are expected to merge into one giant galaxy.
Time doesn't flow the same everywhere. Physicists say there's no single "now" for the whole universe.
Two people moving differently can disagree about whether two events happened at the same time, and both be right.
Space and time are linked into one thing: spacetime.
Mass bends spacetime, and that bending is what we feel as gravity.
Even light bends when it passes heavy objects like galaxies. It's called gravitational lensing.
Einstein's E = mc² means a tiny amount of mass holds a huge amount of energy.
The Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of its mass into energy every second.
Absolute zero, −273.15 °C, is the coldest temperature possible.
Most of an atom is empty space.
If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea in the centre.
Every second, trillions of neutrinos from the Sun pass through your body without touching anything.
There are more possible orders for a deck of 52 cards than there are atoms on Earth.
A shuffled deck of cards is almost certainly in an order that has never existed before in history.
Your body replaces many of its cells over time, but not all of them. Some brain and heart cells stay with you for life.
Scientists think the universe may keep expanding forever, getting colder and darker over unimaginably long times.
The time it takes light to cross the Milky Way is about 100,000 years.
It would take Voyager 1 tens of thousands of years just to pass the nearest star.
The universe has no edge that we know of.
The early universe was smooth, but tiny ripples grew into all the galaxies we see today.
The Sun is just one of hundreds of billions of stars in the Milky Way.
There are likely hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps up to two trillion, in the observable universe.
The light from the most distant galaxies seen by the James Webb Space Telescope left them more than 13 billion years ago.
The word "planet" comes from a Greek word meaning "wanderer," because planets wander across the sky.
Calendars once disagreed so much that when Britain switched to the Gregorian calendar in 1752, it skipped 11 days.
The Julian calendar drifted by about one day every 128 years. That's why it was replaced.
A day on Earth used to be shorter. Earth's spin is slowly slowing down because of the Moon's tides.
The Moon's tides are slowly lengthening Earth's day, by a tiny fraction of a millisecond each century.
Time zones only became standard in the late 1800s, largely because of railway schedules.
India uses a single time zone, five and a half hours ahead of UTC.
Clocks run "clockwise" because they copied the way a sundial's shadow moves in the Northern Hemisphere.
The universe is so old, yet the first humans appeared only about 300,000 years ago.
If you could travel at light speed, from your point of view the trip would take no time at all.
Physicists still don't fully know what time is. It's one of science's biggest open questions.
Entropy, the tendency of things to get more disordered, is one reason time seems to have a direction.
You can remember the past but not the future, and physicists link that to entropy too.
Dark energy, the mysterious force pushing the universe apart, makes up about 68% of everything.
Dark matter can't be seen, but its gravity holds galaxies together.
Without dark matter, galaxies would spin so fast they'd fly apart.
