# Time & universe — batch 6: cosmic questions & life in the universe
Life on Earth is made mostly of six elements: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur.
Carbon is great for life because it can form four strong bonds and build long chains.
Water is special because it stays liquid over a wide range of temperatures.
Amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, have been found in meteorites.
Amino acids have also been found in samples from the asteroid Ryugu and Bennu.
The famous Miller-Urey experiment in 1952 made amino acids from simple chemicals and sparks.
Nobody knows exactly how life began on Earth. It's one of science's biggest mysteries.
Some scientists think life may have begun at deep-sea hydrothermal vents.
Tardigrades and some bacteria can survive conditions that would kill most life, raising hopes for life elsewhere.
Life has been found in places once thought impossible: boiling springs, acidic lakes and deep inside rocks.
Some microbes live kilometres below Earth's surface.
Scientists call organisms that love extreme conditions "extremophiles."
Mars may once have had conditions suitable for microbes.
Methane on Mars has puzzled scientists because it can come from geology or from life.
Europa and Enceladus are top targets in the search for life beyond Earth.
NASA's Europa Clipper launched in 2024 to study Jupiter's icy moon.
The European JUICE mission is on its way to study Jupiter's icy moons.
Titan's chemistry might be similar to early Earth's, frozen in time.
The Dragonfly mission plans to fly a drone-like lander on Titan.
Venus's clouds have been studied for possible signs of life, but nothing has been confirmed.
Astronomers look for "biosignatures," gases like oxygen and methane together, in exoplanet atmospheres.
The James Webb Space Telescope can study the atmospheres of some exoplanets.
No confirmed signal from alien life has ever been detected.
The "Wow! signal" in 1977 was a strong radio signal that was never detected again.
SETI, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence, has been listening for decades.
Earth has been "broadcasting" radio and TV signals into space for about a century.
Our oldest radio signals have travelled only about 100 light-years, a tiny bubble in the galaxy.
The galaxy is about 100,000 light-years wide, so most of it hasn't "heard" us yet.
The Voyager golden records include greetings in 55 languages, including Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Rajasthani and Urdu.
The Voyager records also include music from around the world, including an Indian raga.
The Pioneer probes carry plaques showing where Earth is and what humans look like.
The Arecibo telescope in Puerto Rico collapsed in 2020.
China's FAST is the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world, 500 metres across.
India's GMRT near Pune is one of the world's largest radio telescope arrays operating at low frequencies.
The Square Kilometre Array, being built in Australia and South Africa, will be the world's largest radio telescope.
Some scientists think intelligent life might be rare because so many lucky steps were needed for us.
Others think life is common, but intelligence or technology may be rare.
The "Great Filter" is an idea that something stops most life from becoming space-faring.
The Kardashev scale ranks civilisations by how much energy they can use.
Humans aren't even a Type 1 civilisation on the Kardashev scale yet.
A Dyson sphere is a hypothetical structure that could capture a star's energy.
Astronomers once noticed a star, Tabby's Star, dimming strangely. The likely cause is dust, not aliens.
Fast radio bursts are millisecond flashes of radio waves from distant galaxies. Some come from magnetars.
Some fast radio bursts repeat, and scientists are still working out why.
The universe seems "fine-tuned": small changes to some physical constants might make life impossible.
Some explain fine-tuning with the multiverse idea: we live in the universe that happens to allow us.
The anthropic principle says we can only observe a universe that allows observers to exist.
Our galaxy may contain billions of Earth-sized planets in habitable zones.
A planet in the habitable zone isn't automatically habitable. It needs the right atmosphere too.
The Moon helps stabilise Earth's tilt, which may help keep our climate steady.
Jupiter may help protect Earth from some comets, though it can also send some our way.
Earth's magnetic field protects us from much of the solar wind.
Mars likely lost much of its atmosphere after its magnetic field weakened.
The atmosphere on Earth is extremely thin compared with the planet. It's like the skin on an apple.
Most of Earth's air is in the bottom 10 km or so.
If Earth were the size of a basketball, most of its air would be a layer about as thin as a sheet of paper.
From space, Earth's atmosphere looks like a thin blue line on the horizon.
Every person who has ever lived, as far as we know, lived on this one small planet.
The "Pale Blue Dot" photo shows Earth as a speck less than a pixel wide.
Earth is the only known place in the universe with life, for now.
The distance from the Sun to the edge of the heliosphere, the Sun's bubble, is over 120 times the Earth–Sun distance.
Voyager 1 left the heliosphere in 2012, entering interstellar space.
Voyager 2 left the heliosphere in 2018.
The Voyagers' power is slowly running out. Instruments are being switched off to save energy.
Some light you see at night left its star before humans existed.
You are made of atoms forged in stars billions of years ago, and your atoms will go on long after you.
The universe has been expanding for 13.8 billion years, and every part of you came along for the ride.
Every carbon atom in your body was made inside a star.
Some of the atoms in your body may once have been part of a dinosaur.
You breathe in molecules that were once breathed by people throughout history, because air mixes over time.
When you look up at the night sky, you're looking at your cosmic ancestors.
The cosmos is within us. We are made of star-stuff, as Carl Sagan put it.
We're on a planet orbiting an ordinary star in an ordinary galaxy, yet we can understand the universe. That's pretty remarkable.
