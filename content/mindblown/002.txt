# Mind-blowing — batch 2: history & inventions
The ancient Egyptians used toothpaste made from ingredients like crushed eggshells and ash.
The pyramids of Giza were built by paid workers, not slaves, according to modern archaeology.
The Great Pyramid was originally covered in smooth white limestone that made it shine.
The Colosseum in Rome could hold an estimated 50,000 spectators.
Ancient Romans used concrete so durable that some of their structures still stand after 2,000 years.
The Library of Alexandria was one of the largest libraries of the ancient world.
Vikings reached North America around 500 years before Columbus.
The Great Wall of China was built over many centuries, not all at once.
Paper was invented in China around 2,000 years ago.
The printing press, developed by Gutenberg around 1440, changed how fast ideas could spread.
Napoleon wasn't actually short. He was around average height for his time.
The Eiffel Tower was meant to be temporary and was nearly torn down in 1909.
The Statue of Liberty was a gift from France to the USA in 1886.
The first Olympic Games were held in ancient Greece in 776 BCE, according to tradition.
The first modern Olympics were held in Athens in 1896.
The Titanic sank on its very first voyage, in April 1912.
The Titanic's wreck wasn't found until 1985.
The Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903 lasted only 12 seconds.
Just 66 years after the first powered flight, humans walked on the Moon.
The first email was sent in 1971.
The first text message said "Merry Christmas." It was sent in 1992.
The World Wide Web was invented by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989.
The first website is still online.
The first mobile phone call was made in 1973.
The first iPhone was released in 2007.
Penicillin was discovered by accident in 1928 when Alexander Fleming noticed mould killing bacteria.
X-rays were discovered by accident in 1895.
The microwave oven was invented after an engineer noticed a radar machine had melted a chocolate bar in his pocket.
Post-it notes came from a "failed" glue that wasn't sticky enough.
Bubble wrap was originally invented as textured wallpaper.
Play-Doh was first sold as a wallpaper cleaner.
Coca-Cola was first sold as a medicine in 1886.
Ketchup was sold as a medicine in the 1830s.
Chocolate was once used as money by the Aztecs, in the form of cacao beans.
Salt was so valuable in ancient times that it was used like currency.
Tea was discovered in China, according to legend, when leaves blew into a pot of boiling water.
India is the birthplace of the decimal system that the world uses today.
Aryabhata, an Indian mathematician, calculated pi to four decimal places around 499 CE.
Sushruta, an ancient Indian surgeon, described plastic surgery procedures over 2,000 years ago.
Yoga originated in ancient India.
Takshashila, in ancient India, is often called one of the world's earliest universities.
Nalanda University in Bihar attracted students from across Asia for centuries.
Diamonds were first mined in India, which was the world's only known source for centuries.
The Kohinoor diamond was mined in India, likely from the Golconda region.
The ancient Indus Valley cities had planned streets and drainage systems over 4,000 years ago.
Cotton was first spun and woven in the Indian subcontinent thousands of years ago.
Buttons were first used in the Indus Valley Civilisation, mostly as ornaments.
The game of ludo is based on an Indian game called pachisi.
Kabaddi is believed to have originated in ancient India.
Badminton got its modern rules from a game British officers played in India, called poona.
The ruler was once a valuable measuring tool made from bone, wood or ivory.
The fork wasn't commonly used in Europe until the 1600s.
Ancient Greeks believed the brain was used to cool the blood.
The first known recipe is for beer, from ancient Mesopotamia.
The ancient Romans had a goddess of sewers, Cloacina.
In ancient Egypt, cats were sacred, and harming one could bring severe punishment.
Cleopatra wasn't Egyptian by descent. She was from a Greek-Macedonian family.
Julius Caesar was once kidnapped by pirates and demanded they raise his ransom.
Albert Einstein was offered the presidency of Israel in 1952. He declined.
Isaac Newton developed calculus and his theory of gravity partly while home during a plague outbreak.
Marie Curie was the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
C.V. Raman won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930, the first Asian to win a Nobel in science.
Rabindranath Tagore was the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, in 1913.
Tagore wrote the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh.
Srinivasa Ramanujan had almost no formal training in maths, yet made extraordinary discoveries.
The number 1729 is called the Hardy-Ramanujan number, the smallest number expressible as a sum of two cubes in two different ways.
Mahatma Gandhi was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize five times but never won.
The Taj Mahal took about 20 years to build.
The Taj Mahal changes colour throughout the day, looking pinkish at dawn and golden at sunset.
The Qutub Minar is the tallest brick minaret in the world.
The iron pillar in Delhi has resisted rust for about 1,600 years.
The Konark Sun Temple was designed as a giant chariot with 24 carved stone wheels.
The wheels of the Konark Sun Temple work as sundials.
The Kailasa temple at Ellora was carved from a single rock, from the top down.
Hampi was once one of the richest cities in the world, the capital of the Vijayanagara Empire.
India's first train ran between Bombay and Thane in 1853.
